What paper patient cards really cost a practice

A card that only lives in one drawer works until someone else needs it, or until it is not where it should be. This article stays with the Moroccan practice day, Law 09-08, and a short checklist to start moving to digital records.

26 Nov 20264 min read

A card like this works well for the person who wrote it, on a day when it is in the drawer. The trouble starts when a colleague needs it, when the patient comes back years later, or when the card is left on a desk. We do not sell foreign hospital statistics as if they measured your cabinet. What a paper day costs in the practice

Time spent looking for a card while the patient waits. Questions the patient answers again because last year's notes are not there. Handwriting that only its writer can read. A prescription written by hand, with no check against the allergies on the card. An invoice number that has to be tracked in a second book.

None of that appears on a fee schedule. It shows up as minutes at the start of each visit, and as risk when a detail is missing.

What Moroccan law already asks

Under Law 09-08, data relating to health is sensitive personal data. Processing it needs the patient's express consent and the security measures the law requires. Fines for processing health data without consent, or without the required security, run into tens or hundreds of thousands of dirhams. 1

Paper in a drawer is not automatic compliance. Encryption, roles, a log of who opened what, and a way to export or erase a patient's data are tools a practice needs when it moves the record onto a computer. Our article on Bill 52-26 explains what may come next, and what is already in force.

A practical checklist to start digitising

Going digital does not have to happen in one weekend. These steps keep the practice running while the paper goes.

  1. Count what you have: how many active patient cards, and how many in the archive. This tells you the size of the job.
  2. Start with active patients: those in treatment or with an appointment in the coming months. The archive can follow later.
  3. Agree how a patient is found: by name, phone number or insurance number. Write it down so everyone files the same way.
  4. Keep the original handwriting. Scan or photograph each card and attach it to the patient's digital record, so nothing written by hand is lost.
  5. From an agreed day, every new patient and every new appointment goes straight into the software. Older cards move over as those patients come back.
  6. Agree who enters what. Reception and dentists should know which part of the record is theirs.
  7. Protect the data from the first day: the software should encrypt it, limit who can see it and back it up on its own.
  8. Set a date after which no new paper card is started.
