What paper patient cards really cost a practice

A paper card is cheap to print and quick to fill in. Its real cost shows up later, in the details nobody can find, read or trust when they are needed.

26 Nov 20265 min read

The card below comes from a dental practice in Morocco. On one side, a printed tooth chart. Next to it, a handwritten table of dates, teeth, treatments, agreed prices and amounts received. It holds a patient's whole history on a single sheet of paper.

A card like this works well for the person who wrote it, on a day when it is in the drawer. The trouble starts when someone else needs it, or when it is not where it should be. This article looks at what research says paper records cost in missing information, lost files, errors and time. It ends with a short checklist for practices that want to start moving to digital records.

The studies come from other countries. We cite each one so you can judge it for yourself.

Information that is missing when it is needed

At three UK teaching hospitals that all used paper records, key clinical information was missing in 15% of outpatient consultations. 1 Of those patients, 32% had a delay or disruption to their care, and 20% were put at risk of harm. 1

In the United States, a study of 1,614 primary care visits in Colorado found the same pattern. Clinicians reported that clinical information was missing in 13.6% of visits, nearly 1 in 7. 2 They judged the missing information at least somewhat likely to harm the patient 44% of the time. 2 Missing information was 60% less likely when the clinician had full electronic records. 2

A small Italian study points the same way for dentistry. Twenty dental students reviewed 200 patient records, half on paper and half digital. With the paper records they missed 31 important items: 24 patients taking antiresorptive medication, 6 on anticoagulants and one penicillin allergy. With the digital records, no oversights were detected. 3 The sample is small, but these are the details that matter before surgery or a prescription.

Files that cannot be found

An audit at a university dental hospital in South Africa set out to review 362 patient records. 105 of them, about 29%, could not be retrieved, so only 257 were evaluated. 4 The authors put this down to file numbers that could not be read in the admissions book, and to files that were lost. 4

A paper card exists in one place only. Filed under the wrong name, left on a desk or taken to another room, it is missing for everyone else. There is no search box for a drawer.

Searching also costs time. In 50 hours of observation of junior doctors on call at two UK teaching hospitals, 13% of their time went on searching, about 30 minutes in every 4 hours. Most of it was spent looking for paperwork and equipment. 5 A dental practice is not a hospital, but the scene is familiar: the patient is in the chair while someone looks for the card.

Handwriting that only its writer can read

Handwriting is fast for the person writing and slow for everyone else. On the card above, short abbreviations make sense to the dentist who wrote them. A colleague, a replacement or the same dentist two years later may read them differently.

Prescriptions show the effect clearly. In a study of 12 community practices in New York, prescription errors among the providers who switched to electronic prescribing fell from 42.5% to 6.6% after one year, about seven times fewer. 6 Among those who kept writing by hand, errors rose slightly, from 37.3% to 38.4%. 6 Electronic prescribing also removed illegibility problems completely. 6

The cost of medication errors is large at every scale. The World Health Organization puts their global cost at an estimated US$42 billion a year. 7

What it adds up to

None of these costs appears on an invoice. They show up as minutes lost at the start of an appointment, a question the patient has to answer again, a treatment that cannot be checked against last year's, or an amount nobody can confirm. Each one is small. Together, they decide how much of the day goes on care and how much on paper.

A practical checklist to start digitising

Going digital does not have to happen in one weekend. These steps keep the practice running while the paper goes.

  1. Count what you have: how many active patient cards, and how many in the archive. This tells you the size of the job.
  2. Start with active patients: those in treatment or with an appointment in the coming months. The archive can follow later.
  3. Agree how a patient is found: by name, phone number or insurance number. Write it down so everyone files the same way.
  4. Keep the original handwriting. Scan or photograph each card and attach it to the patient's digital record, so nothing written by hand is lost.
  5. From an agreed day, every new patient and every new appointment goes straight into the software. Older cards move over as those patients come back.
  6. Agree who enters what. Reception and dentists should know which part of the record is theirs.
  7. Protect the data from the first day: the software should encrypt it, limit who can see it and back it up on its own.
  8. Set a date after which no new paper card is started.
