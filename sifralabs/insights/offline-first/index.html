Why practice software should keep working without internet

A practice runs on its calendar and its patient files. If either one depends on a connection or a server somewhere else, a cut line or an attack can stop the whole day.

26 Nov 20264 min read

When you choose practice software, it is easy to compare screens and prices. It is harder to ask what happens on a bad day: when the internet is down, when a computer breaks, or when someone tries to lock or steal your data. This article stays with Morocco and with design you can check yourself.

When the connection drops

Software that lives on a remote server needs the internet to open a patient file or the day's calendar. Lines get cut, routers fail and providers have outages. When that happens, the patient is still in the chair.

Software that runs on the practice's own computers keeps working through all of that. The internet stays useful for updates and mail, but the day no longer depends on it.

Morocco is not spared

In April 2025, a cyberattack on the National Social Security Fund (CNSS) allegedly exposed information on nearly 2 million people, according to Moroccan media reports. 1 Health and social data is a target here too.

Law 09-08 already treats health data as sensitive and asks for security measures when you process it. 2 Keeping the record on your own computers, encrypted, with a backup you control, is one practical answer.

The lesson is not that every practice will be attacked. It is that a backup on the same network as the computer it protects may not survive an attack, and that stolen data should be unreadable to whoever takes it.

Three design rules

Local first

The data lives on the practice's own computers, and every daily task works without internet: the calendar, the patient file, the invoice, the backup.

Encrypted at rest

The database is encrypted on disk, and the key is kept apart from the data. A stolen laptop or a copied file is useless without it.

Backups that happen by themselves

A backup that depends on someone remembering it will be forgotten on a busy week. Backups should run on their own, be encrypted, be able to leave the building on an external drive, and keep older copies for a while.

How we designed MeDoc Dentist

We applied these rules to MeDoc Dentist, the dental practice program we make and sell in Morocco.

  • MeDoc Dentist runs entirely on the practice's own computers. Appointments, charts, invoices and backups work without an internet connection, and patient data is not sent to a cloud.
  • The whole practice database is encrypted on disk with SQLCipher (AES-256). The key stays in the computer's secure keychain, not next to the data.
  • It backs up the practice once a day by itself, to a USB stick or an external drive if you choose one. Each backup is compressed, then encrypted with AES-256-GCM, and X-rays and scans are included.
  • It keeps the last 3 days of backups, one from last week and one from the last half-year, and deletes older ones automatically.
  • Every backup carries a cryptographic signature. A backup that was altered or damaged is flagged and cannot be restored.
  • Restoring takes one click. Before anything is replaced, MeDoc Dentist saves a copy of today's data, and puts it back if the restore fails.
  • In the Cabinet and Cabinet+ editions, the reception computer and the treatment-room computers share one database over the practice's local network, encrypted and with every change signed. A computer that loses the connection keeps working and catches up when it is back.

There are limits, and we say them plainly. The daily backup runs while the program is open, not in the background. And a backup drive only protects you from an attack if it is unplugged afterwards and kept somewhere else.

A checklist to test your own setup

  1. On a quiet day, unplug the internet for an hour. Can you still open the calendar, a patient file and an invoice?
  2. Ask where your patient data is stored: on your own computers, or on a server somewhere else. Who else can reach it?
  3. Ask whether the data is encrypted on disk, and where the key is kept.
  4. Find out when the last backup ran, and where it is. If nobody knows, assume there is none.
  5. Keep one copy off the network: a drive that is unplugged after the backup and stored in another place.
  6. Try a restore once, on a spare computer, before the day you need it.
  7. Write down who to call when something goes wrong, and how quickly they answer.
